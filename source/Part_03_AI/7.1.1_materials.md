## What is a Computer? 
A **computer** is a machine that can store information and be programmed to automatically carry out sequences of arithmetic or logical operations. 

To accomplish this, computers rely on four primary components:
1. **Memory:** Microchips that store data. 
2. **Computation Hardware:** Microprocessors that perform the arithmetic and logical operations.
3. **Input Devices:** Hardware used to receive information.
4. **Output Devices:** Hardware used to present the results of the computation.

## Evolution of Computing Hardware
To understand how modern computers operate, it is helpful to look at how computer hardware has evolved. Early computers relied on different physical methods to create the on and off switches required for computation.

* **Mechanical Relays (1930s):** Early computers, such as the Model K built in 1937, used mechanical relays and scrap materials to perform simple binary addition. Because these relays required physical movement to close a switch, they suffered from material failure over time and were typically built to accomplish only a single specific task.
* **Vacuum Tubes (1940s):** Engineers replaced mechanical switches with vacuum tubes (similar to light bulbs) that could turn on and off using a small amount of energy without moving parts. The ENIAC was the world's first electronic programmable computer. It weighed 30 tons and consumed massive amounts of energy, with most power lost as heat. The tubes burned out frequently and could not be scaled down in size.
* **Silicon Semiconductors (Modern):** Modern computers use microscopic transistors made from semiconductor materials like silicon. These modern transistors have no moving parts, last significantly longer, and can be scaled down to fit billions of switches onto a single computer chip.

## Transistors and Data Storage
At the lowest physical level, a computer chip is composed of billions of transistors made from semiconductor materials like silicon. 

Transistors act as microscopic on and off switches. A computer operates by changing the specific path that electricity takes through these transistors. 

### Bits and Bytes
Digital files are saved to a specific physical location on a computer chip. Data is recorded based on the state of the transistors:
* **Bit:** A single transistor state. "On" represents a 1, and "off" represents a 0.
* **Byte:** A grouping of eight (8) bits. 

Because a single byte contains eight distinct bits, it can store 256 different values ($2^8 = 256$).
Examples of these values include: 
- 00000000 = 0
- 00000001 = $2^0 = 1$
- 00000010 = $2^1 = 2$
- 01010101 = $2^6 + 2^4 + 2^2 + 2^ 0 = 64 + 16 + 4 + 1 = 85$


Computers use these binary values to encode all types of data, mapping numbers to text characters (using ASCII), pixel colors for images, and sampled sound wave amplitudes for audio.

## Logic Gates and Operations
To perform operations and math, transistors are wired together into specific patterns called logic gates. These gates take one or more binary inputs and produce a specific binary output based on logical rules. 

The four primary logic gates are:
* **AND Gate:** Outputs a 1 only if *both* inputs are 1.
* **OR Gate:** Outputs a 1 as long as *at least one* input is a 1.
* **XOR (Exclusive OR) Gate:** Outputs a 1 if *one and only one* input is a 1.
* **NAND Gate:** Outputs a 1 as long as both inputs are *not* 1.

By connecting these gates in parallel and series, computers can add binary numbers together, perform linear algebra, and execute complex programming commands. Programming a computer is simply the act of telling the system which path the energy should take through these logical operations. 

Because adding even small binary numbers requires wiring multiple logic gates together, modern computers rely on billions of microscopic transistors to process complex calculations, a concept you will explore further during your in-class activities.

### Simulating Logic in Excel
Engineers can simulate the behavior of physical transistors using software tools. Excel features built-in logical operators such as `=AND()`, `=OR()`, and `=XOR()`. 

Unlike physical computer hardware that outputs 1s and 0s, Excel's logical operators report outputs as either `TRUE` or `FALSE`. To convert these text outputs into binary numerical values, you must wrap the logical operator in an `=IF()` function. The `=IF()` function can be programmed to report a 1 if the logical operator is true, and a 0 if the logical operator is false.
